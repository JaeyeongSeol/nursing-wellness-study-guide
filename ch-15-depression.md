## 2. Risk factors

The slides group risks. They do not print the headings “modifiable” and “non-modifiable.” The split below follows what can change with care versus what the slide lists as demographic or historical.

### Demographic and psychosocial (Slide 5)

**Not presented as something the nurse can change**

- Female gender
- Personal or familial history

**Psychosocial influences the intervention slides try to address**

- Bereavement / loss of significant relationships
- Loneliness
- Recent social stressors
- Stressful social environment
- Loss of meaningful social interaction
- Lack of social supports
- Loss of significant roles
- Current or previous experiences of abuse and neglect
- Being a caregiver, including primary care of a grandchild

Textbook note: stressors alone are not the whole risk. The book says the combination of stressors and the absence of social supports increases risk. Social support, relationship quality, and confidants are protective. That protective point is textbook, and it matches the slide interventions on socialization and support groups.

### Medical conditions and functional impairments (Slide 6)

- Many conditions raise risk (examples: cancer, dementia, Parkinson’s, MS/ALS)
- Disability can lead to depression
- Depression can lead to other medical problems
- Depression worsens pain and pain worsens depression
- Nutritional deficiencies can be both a risk factor and an effect

### Medications, alcohol, and cognition (Slide 7)

- Adverse medication effects can induce a depression that improves or disappears when the medication is stopped
- Adverse effects can simulate depression (lethargy, insomnia, irritability)
- Withdrawal of certain medications can cause a depressive episode
- Older adults are more sensitive to adverse effects because of age-related changes
- Alcohol and depression have a circular relationship
- Cognitive impairments and dementia

### Suicide risk factors the nurse assesses (Slide 26)

Personal and family history of depression, past suicide attempts, loneliness, family discord, feelings of abandonment, recent bereavement, presence of chronic or severe pain.

## 3. High-priority points

- **Same illness, different picture** (Slides 3–4). Older adults are less likely to admit depressive symptoms, more likely to have depression with other conditions, more likely to have serious consequences, and more likely to be unreported and undertreated.

| Younger adults | Older adults |
| -------------- | ------------ |
| More emotional symptoms | More cognitive and physical (somatic) symptoms |
| Hopelessness, uselessness, helplessness | Apathy; exaggeration of personal helplessness |
| Negative feelings toward self | Sense of emptiness; withdrawal from social activities |
| Insomnia | Hypersomnia; early-morning awakening |
| Eating disorders | Anorexia; weight loss |
| More talk of suicidal ideation than attempts; more passive means | Less talk about suicide; more likely to attempt; more violent means |

- **Functional consequences, physical** (Slide 8): loss of appetite, weight loss, GI symptoms, insomnia, hypersomnia, early awakening, fatigue, loss of energy, pain, loss of libido, slowed or increased psychomotor activity.
- **Functional consequences, psychosocial** (Slide 9): sad, low, or flat affect; absence of feelings; low life satisfaction; low self-esteem and passivity; inattention to appearance; slowed thinking and poor memory; exaggeration of mental deficits; rumination; guilt; hopelessness; unworthiness; uselessness.
- **Eight intervention categories** (Slide 13), in the slide’s order:
  1. Alleviate risk factors (discuss medication alternatives with the primary provider; refer alcohol use to AA or other counselling; improve function with continence products, sensory aids, mobility aids).
  2. Improve psychosocial function (autonomy, self-efficacy, decisions about daily care; socialization; support groups for caregiving, widowhood, loss of children).
  3. Physical activity and nutrition (group or individually tailored exercise; in hospital, refer to PT, OT, and recreational therapy).
  4. Education and counselling (identify fears and give reality-based information; help the person verbalize emotion; story-telling that includes strengths; active listening and empathy about losses).
  5. Refer for psychosocial therapies and geriatric mental health or geriatric assessment for major depression.
  6. Antidepressant teaching and management.
  7. Complementary and alternative interventions.
  8. Teaching about ECT.
- **Start low and go slow.** Antidepressants can take several weeks to show effect (Slide 20).
- **Contraindicated in the elderly, as written on the slide:** amitriptyline, doxepine, and fluoxetine (Slide 20).
- **Tricyclics are not first-line** because of side effects and because overdose is lethal (Slide 22). Worst effects in the elderly: cardiovascular (orthostatic hypotension with rate and rhythm disturbances) and anticholinergic (blurred vision, urinary retention, cognitive impairment). Also constipation, dry mouth, sedation, and weight gain. People with glaucoma, prostatic hyperplasia, or cardiac conduction abnormalities should not take them.
- **ECT is considered when** antidepressants are ineffective, not tolerated, or medically risky, or when health is significantly compromised by depression (Slide 24).
- **Suicide attempts in later life are more often lethal.** If thoughts are suspected, probe further. A detailed plan plus access to means is high risk and needs immediate action (Slide 26).
- **Closing idea** (Slide 28): psychosocial and spiritual care is how care is provided, not only what care is provided.

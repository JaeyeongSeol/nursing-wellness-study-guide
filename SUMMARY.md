## Chapters 1–2 — Aging, ageism, culture

**Four ages**

| Term | Meaning |
| ---- | ------- |
| Subjective age | The person’s own perception of their age |
| Perceived age | Other people’s estimation of someone’s age |
| Chronologic age | Time since birth |
| Functional age | Physiological health, psychological well-being, socioeconomic factors, and ability to function and take part in desired activities |

Aging involves physical, psychological, and social changes. It is normal and includes losses **and** gains.

**Ageism** is a negative attitude toward aging and older people (bias or prejudice related to age). Institutional ageism is systemic: cultural assumptions, and hidden bias in policy that is dismissed as “that’s just the way it is.” In health care it affects policy, insurance, and treatment. Common ailments get less aggressive treatment and are blamed on aging even when they are reversible.

Effects named but **not defined**: aging anxiety, age attribution, anti-aging movement.

Counter it: notice your own and institutional bias, have positive interactions, learn enough to correct misconceptions.

**Gerontology** is the scientific, holistic study of the effects of time on human development (healthy and successful aging, diversity). **Geriatrics** is the medical subspecialty focused on medical problems of older people. It has shifted from curing to caring, with emphasis on quality of life. Gerontological nursing is core knowledge for every nurse. Care of older people is the fastest-growing employment segment in health care.

**Cultural continuum:** awareness (values of client and self) → sensitivity (differences exist) → recognition (different customs and behaviours) → competence (enough knowledge to interact with respect and understanding). **Cultural safety** is the recipient’s experience: identity is not denied or challenged.

About 20% of Canadians identify as a visible minority (2/3 immigrated, 1/3 born in Canada). Largest older-adult groups on the slide: South Asian, Chinese, Southeast Asian, Canadian Indigenous.

- South Asian: cultural barriers to health services
- Chinese: often in good health; barriers are cultural and linguistic
- Southeast Asian: higher cervical cancer (Vietnamese), higher type 2 diabetes (Filipino), poorer health partly from war trauma
- Indigenous: First Nations 60%+, Métis 30%+, Inuit ~4%. About 630 First Nations communities. About 53 First Nations long-term care facilities. Shorter life expectancy linked to unemployment, poor housing, and social issues. Only about 6% of Aboriginal peoples are over 65.

Generalizations can be useful. They cannot replace individualized care. Avoid stigmatizing.

**Book:** cultural competence is also described as an ongoing application of knowledge, skills, and attitudes (CNA/RNAO). The book’s competence ladder (unconsciously incompetent → unconsciously competent) is not the slide continuum. Butler’s ageism definition is longer than the slide sentence. Use the slide.

## Chapters 5–7 — Promotion, settings, assessment

**Age-related changes** are inevitable, progressive, and irreversible, and happen to everyone. Physically degenerative. Psychological and spiritual side can still grow. They are inherent processes that increase vulnerability.

**Risk factors** are conditions that increase vulnerability: disease, environment, lifestyle, support systems, psychosocial circumstances, adverse medication effects, attitudes based on lack of knowledge. The wellness figure also lists pathologic conditions, physiologic and psychologic stressors, environmental barriers, ageist attitudes, and lack of information.

**Functional consequences** are the observable effects of actions, risk factors, and age-related changes on quality of life or daily activities.

- Positive: highest function, least dependency, best quality of life
- Negative: interfere with function or quality of life (more vulnerability, less health, less quality of life)

Wellness-model interventions: address risk factors, teach health promotion, refer. Outcomes: more safety and function, more quality of life. Keep the whole person and environment in view (mind, body, and spirit).

Health promotion: prevention and screening, risk reduction, environmental modification, health education.

**Where care happens**

| Setting | Focus | Nurse |
| ------- | ----- | ----- |
| Community | Health promotion, respite, adult day centres | Education, referrals |
| Home | Goal is rehabilitation. Meals, personal care, errands, light housekeeping | Education, referrals, assessment, care planning, hands-on care |
| Nursing home | Needs 24-hour help with ADLs | Those same roles, plus continuous on-site responsibility and coordination with other professions |
| Hospital | Named as a point of access | |

Functional decline is tied to **transitions in care**, not to one building. The slide does not mark hospital vs home vs long-term care as the answer. Transition problems: communication gaps, incomplete information, poor client education, poor medication reconciliation, language or culture, low staffing.

**Why assessment is hard:** more than one acute and chronic problem; illness and drug effects are obscure; one change can have many causes; treatment often targets symptoms; cognitive impairment affects the history; myths blame “normal aging.”

**Tools.** Purpose: find what belongs on the care plan. They should be evidence-based and easy to use.

| Type | Named tools |
| ---- | ----------- |
| Physical | History first, then head-to-toe, vital signs including orthostatic hypotension |
| Functional | ADLs, IADLs, assistance level (independent, semi-independent, dependent), senses, baseline cognition. Tools: KATZ, 48/6, FHA tool |
| Mental status | CAM, MMSE, GDS |
| Comprehensive | SPICES, MDS |
| Safety | Fall risk, Braden |

48/6: adults 70+, six functional areas, first 48 hours of admission. The six areas are **not named**. KATZ items are **not listed**.

**Book:** ADLs include bathing, dressing, mouth care, hair care, eating, transfers, ambulation, bed mobility, bladder and bowel. IADLs include shopping, laundry, transportation, housekeeping, meals, money, medications, telephone. SPICES = Sleep, Problems with eating, Incontinence, Confusion, Evidence of falls, Skin breakdown.

**Interview:** quiet, lit, comfortable, private, bathroom nearby. Formal name, explain the purpose, speak to the older person, follow that culture’s modesty. Under 30 minutes, no jargon, summarize, warn them the visit is ending.

## Chapter 8 — Medications

| Term | Meaning |
| ---- | ------- |
| Pharmacokinetics | How a drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted |
| Pharmacodynamics | How the drug affects the body at the cell and the target organ |
| Half-life | Time for the concentration to fall by half |

Most oral drugs are absorbed in the small intestine. Absorption changes with less gastric acid, higher gastric pH, slower emptying, and food. **Five half-lives** to reach steady state, and five half-lives to clear the drug after it is stopped.

**Body changes that change drug action:** lower eGFR, lower hepatic blood flow, less muscle and body water, low serum albumin, altered receptor sensitivity.

**Taking the drug depends on:** motivation, knowledge, culture and psychosocial factors, ability to open the container, ability to swallow, and skill with nasal, skin, subcutaneous, and other routes.

**Polypharmacy** means more drugs than are clinically indicated. It is about appropriateness and combination, not the count. Every drug has side effects. Older adults often feel them more strongly. Ask whether benefit outweighs risk.

**Higher adverse-event risk:** more drugs, malnutrition or dehydration, several comorbidities, illness that hits heart, kidney, or liver, cognitive impairment, past allergy or adverse effect, fever, a recent health change. Watch anticoagulants/antiplatelets, antidiabetics, NSAIDs, and CNS drugs.

**Anticholinergics** block acetylcholine and inhibit the parasympathetic system. Sources include cough, cold, flu, and sleep products, plus antihistamines, antidepressants, antipsychotics, cardiac drugs, antiparkinsonian drugs, and GI or urinary drugs. They contribute to long-term cognitive impairment and can cancel cholinesterase inhibitors used for dementia. Safer alternatives usually exist.

**Beers list:** drugs that raise the risk of delirium and other adverse reactions. Do not use them in older adults unless benefit clearly outweighs risk, and then for the shortest time.

**Antipsychotics** are used more in long-term care. In the US the FDA limits them to psychotic symptoms, not wandering, anxiety, or insomnia. The slide says Canada is not the same.

**Tardive dyskinesia:** rhythmic involuntary movements of trunk, limbs, jaw, lips, mouth, or tongue (wormlike tongue movements, chewing, grimacing, lip smacking, jaw clenching, blinking). Can start 3–6 months after an antipsychotic and **continue after the drug is stopped**.

**Drug-induced parkinsonism:** shaking and poorly controlled movement. Easy to mistake for Parkinson disease. **Can reverse if the drug is stopped.**

Assess regular and PRN drugs by every route, vitamins, supplements, herbs, alcohol, and smoking. Reconcile at every transition: see the full list, check whether prescriptions can be filled, fix adherence problems, let the person ask questions. Keep a current list, teach PRN use, use pillboxes or blister packs, try non-drug approaches for behaviour, reduce the number of drugs, and check cost. Pharmacist reviews interactions. Seniors Clinic, home care, and social work are the referral paths.

## Chapters 9–10 — Law, ethics, abuse

| Term | Meaning |
| ---- | ------- |
| Competency | Legal ability to fulfill one’s role and handle affairs responsibly |
| Decision-making capacity | Ability to make an informed, logical decision about one health-care matter |
| Partial guardianship | The person still makes limited decisions |
| Full guardianship | The person loses all decision rights |
| Trusteeship | Financial decisions only |
| Advance directive | Legally binding document, made while competent, about care wanted or refused if the person later cannot decide or communicate |
| Physical restraint | Any device, method, or equipment that immobilizes or reduces free movement of arms, legs, body, or head |
| ANH | Bypassing the upper GI tract to deliver nutrition |

Advance-directive examples on the slide: MOST, and a representation agreement / living will (usually end of life). Nursing-home rights in Canada are mainly provincial or territorial. The ethical tension is safety versus freedom.

Restraints are tied to fractures, delirium, soft-tissue injury, and death. They limit autonomy and dignity. ANH methods named: PEG, jejunostomy, NG, TPN by central line, hypodermoclysis.

**MAiD:** Bill C-14, June 2016. The Criminal Code allows a person, in limited circumstances, to request a substance intended to end their life. Only two forms are permitted. The slide does **not** name the two forms or the eligibility list. It does say every criterion must be met, including every criterion for a grievous and irremediable condition. Three system roles: determine eligibility, provide MAiD, aid in providing it. An **RN only aids** a physician or nurse practitioner. An RN may not prescribe, compound, dispense, or administer the substance.

Nurse’s legal and ethical role: promote advance care planning, implement directives, facilitate decisions, support caregiver wellness.

**Senior abuse** is harm by someone the older adult knows and would normally trust.

| Type | Slide definition |
| ---- | ---------------- |
| Physical | Inflict or threaten pain or injury, or deprive the person of a basic need |
| Sexual | Any nonconsensual sexual contact, or coercing the person to witness sexual behaviour |
| Exploitation | Illegal taking, misuse, or concealment of funds, property, or assets |
| Neglect | Refusal or failure by those responsible to provide food, shelter, health care, or protection |
| Emotional | Slide gives signs, not the definition sentence. Signs: low self-esteem, exhaustion, nervousness around one person, depression, anxiety, isolation, fear, withdrawal. Examples: ignoring wishes, disrespecting belongings, speaking another language in front of the person, controlling activities, treating them like a child, bullying, name-calling |
| Spiritual | Denying spiritual practice, privacy for prayer, religious diet, or religious body care (example: cutting a Sikh man’s hair) |
| Abandonment and self-neglect | Named only |

**Book** (the p. 164 block the slides cite): emotional abuse is inflicting mental pain, anguish, or distress by verbal or nonverbal acts. Abandonment is desertion by someone who took responsibility for care or custody. Self-neglect is the person’s own behaviour that threatens health or safety, such as not eating or not taking essential medicines. There is no other perpetrator.

**Perpetrator risks:** mental illness, alcoholism, hostility, financial dependence on the victim. **Victim risks:** dementia, problem behaviours, physical or communication disability, physical dependence, financial dependence, shared living, isolation.

People do not report because they do not see it as abuse, do not know where to get help, fear it will get worse, fear the consequences, feel shame or blame, fear losing the relationship, or cannot communicate. Staff fear revenge, job loss, lack of support, getting someone in trouble, or getting involved, and may not know the law or the procedure.

Report **suspicion**. You do not have to prove it. Police or the public agency verifies. Investigation is usually in **24–72 hours**. Clues: bruises, welts, fractures, frostbite, dehydration, oversedation, mental changes, uncontrolled medical conditions. Good-faith reporters are generally immune. BCCNM Duty to Report comes from the Health Professions Act. Reporting is a professional legal duty.

## Chapters 12–13 — Psychosocial health

**Cognition** is thinking and reasoning. Impairments include delirium and dementia. **Affective function** is inside the self and between the person and others. Impairments include depression, psychiatric illness, and loss of touch with reality.

**Life events:** retirement, relocation, chronic illness and functional loss, widowhood, death of friends and family, ageist attitudes, loss of autonomy (driving is the example).

**Stress** is the sum of all effects of factors acting on the body. Stressors can be ordinary activities or disease, pleasant or unpleasant. Stages: **alarm, resistance, exhaustion**. Chronic stress raises risk of various conditions. The conditions are not named.

**High stress when:** poor health, impaired function, weak supports, little money, immature development, few coping skills, unexpected events, several hassles at once, several major events close together.

**Coping** is problem-focused or emotion-focused: seek information, reframe, stay hopeful, reduce stress, exercise, fantasize about outcomes, get reassurance, set realistic limits, use art, music, or writing.

**Resilience** is bouncing back physically and psychologically, using coping, and growing in wisdom. It is linked to spirituality, hope, and meaning. The nurse’s job is to support it.

Spirituality becomes broader and more personal with age. Practice is linked to positive health outcomes. Know what the belief does for that person. The true/false item about church networks and health is **not answered** on the slides.

Health literacy is listening, speaking health needs, and acting on written instructions. **6 in 10** adult Canadians are not proficient. This is especially true for immigrants.

**MMSE areas:** orientation, attention, memory, language, spatial-visual skills.

| Term | Meaning |
| ---- | ------- |
| Delusion | Fixed false belief |
| Hallucination | Sensory experience with no outside stimulus |
| Illusion | Misperception of a real outside stimulus |

Hallucination clues: extreme withdrawal, being content alone, gestures as if answering a stimulus. Ask about thoughts they cannot dismiss, voices when alone, and seeing what others do not see.

Also assess appearance, movement and body language, social skill, response to the interview, alertness, speech, calculation, decision-making and executive function, mood, supports, and religion.

**Book:** executive dysfunction can show up before memory loss, especially if the frontal lobes are affected. A person can pass the MMSE and still be unsafe in daily life. GAI is a 20-item anxiety screen; 9 or more “agree” answers need further evaluation. The slide only cites p. 240.

Diagnoses to recognize: situational low self-esteem, powerlessness, social isolation, ineffective coping, and readiness for enhanced spiritual well-being, coping, or resilience.

Interventions: protect self-esteem (no baby talk, no elderspeak), give control and privacy, include the person in decisions, find meaningful roles, reminisce, build supports, address spiritual needs, teach healthy aging.

Evaluation: positive self-perception, involvement in decisions, a sense of control, effective coping.

## Chapter 14 — Delirium and dementia

The three Ds are delirium, dementia, and depression.

| | Delirium | Dementia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Acute confusion. Sudden, temporary changes in cognition, attention, memory, and perception | Irreversible loss of cognitive function |
| Speed | Hours to days | Months to years |
| Course | Fluctuates | Progresses |
| Outlook | Preventable and treatable. A medical emergency | Terminal |
| Nursing focus | Risk factors and symptoms | Safety, caregiver strain, quality of life |
| Cause on the slide | Unknown. Thought to be neurotransmitter disturbance with many factors | Damage to or loss of nerve cells and their connections |

**Delirium types:** hyperactive (agitation, hallucinations, restlessness), hypoactive (lethargy, less movement), mixed (switches between them).

**CAM:** need features **1 and 2, plus 3 or 4**.

1. Acute onset or fluctuating course
2. Inattention
3. Disorganized thinking
4. Altered level of consciousness

Drug treatment of delirium is **not** recommended. Review drugs, limit psychoactive drugs to specific symptoms, stop nonessential drugs. Benzodiazepines or psychotropics are used when delirium is from **alcohol withdrawal**. Other care: correct the reversible cause, environment, education, communication. Watch safety and whatever makes it worse.

**Prevalence:** 22% in community-living people (the book says community-living people with dementia), 15–70% in long-term care, 11–33% on hospital admission, another 5–35% after admission, 80% or more in ICU.

Consequences: longer stay, more likely to live in long-term care, short- and long-term functional loss, new or worse dementia.

**Book — predisposing:** advanced age, dementia, depression, functional dependency, number of medications. **Precipitating:** surgery, infections, serious illness, physical restraints. Risk is highest when several predisposing factors meet one or more precipitating factors. The slide names these two groups and does not list them.

**Dementia features named:** aphasia, apraxia, agnosia, disturbed executive function. The slide does not define those four words.

**Types and shares:** Alzheimer 60–80%. Vascular 11–18% (22–34% together with Alzheimer). Lewy body and Parkinson disease 15–20%. Type may be clear only after death. The slide does not print the distinguishing features.

**Book, short:** Alzheimer is insidious and slow, often memory first. Vascular is more abrupt and follows strokes or vascular risks such as hypertension. Lewy body fluctuates, with parkinsonism, hallucinations, and high sensitivity to neuroleptics and anticholinergics. Frontotemporal often starts before 65, family history is common, behaviour or language changes early, and memory loss comes later. Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine are not effective for frontotemporal degeneration. The slide’s “risk factors” heading has no bullets. The book links prevention to the same habits that protect the heart: activity, diet, diabetes, blood pressure, depression treatment, plus cognitive and social activity.

People with dementia keep personhood. In hospital, behaviour worsens because of the acute illness, the unfamiliar place, unfamiliar staff, and broken routines.

Pain observation on the slide (0–2 each, total 0–10): breathing, negative vocalization, facial expression, consolability. The tool is not named.

## Chapter 15 — Depression

Depression is clinically similar at every age. Older adults are less likely to say they are depressed, more likely to have it with other illnesses, more likely to suffer serious consequences, and more likely to be missed and undertreated.

| Younger | Older |
| ------- | ----- |
| Emotional symptoms | Cognitive and physical (somatic) symptoms |
| Hopeless, useless, helpless | Apathy, exaggerated helplessness, emptiness, social withdrawal |
| Negative view of self | |
| Insomnia | Hypersomnia, early-morning waking |
| Eating disorders | Anorexia, weight loss |
| More talk of suicide than attempts; passive means | Less talk; more attempts; more violent means |

**Risks:** female gender, personal or family history, bereavement, loneliness, recent stress, a stressful environment, lost interaction, lost supports, lost roles, abuse or neglect, caregiving (including raising a grandchild). Medical examples: cancer, dementia, Parkinson’s, MS/ALS. Disability can lead to depression, and depression can lead to other medical problems. Pain and depression worsen each other. Poor nutrition can be a cause or a result. A drug can cause depression that lifts when the drug stops, or can imitate it with lethargy, insomnia, and irritability. Stopping some drugs can also trigger an episode. Alcohol and depression feed each other. Cognitive impairment and dementia add risk.

**Physical effects:** appetite and weight loss, GI symptoms, insomnia or hypersomnia, early waking, fatigue, pain, lost libido, slowed or sped-up movement. **Psychosocial effects:** flat affect, no feelings, low life satisfaction, low self-esteem, neglected appearance, slow thinking, poor memory, exaggerated deficits, rumination, guilt, hopelessness, unworthiness, uselessness.

**GDS:** 5–7 minutes, used in many settings, can detect depression, can be used with cognitive impairment, available in 30 other languages. No cutoff score is on the slide.

**Eight interventions, in order:** reduce risks, improve psychosocial function, activity and nutrition, education and counselling, refer for therapy (geriatric mental health for major depression), teach and manage antidepressants, teach alternatives, teach about ECT.

If drugs are the risk, ask the primary provider about alternatives. If alcohol is the risk, refer to AA or other counselling. Also use continence products, sensory aids, and mobility aids. Support autonomy and social contact, including groups for caregiving, widowhood, and loss of a child. In hospital, refer to PT, OT, and recreation. Help the person name fears, tell the story, and hear both strengths and losses.

| Drug | Exam point |
| ---- | ---------- |
| SSRI | First line. Blocks serotonin reuptake. Safer and effective |
| SNRI | Raises serotonin and norepinephrine. Less anticholinergic effect than cyclic drugs |
| Both | **Hyponatremia**, common in older adults. **Start low and go slow.** Effect can take several weeks |
| Do not use | **Amitriptyline, doxepine, fluoxetine** |
| MAOI | Rare. Agitation and psychosis in dementia. Only if other treatment fails, and only with a psychiatrist |
| Tricyclic | Not first line. Helps sleep, appetite, libido, and pleasure, but overdose can kill. Worst problems: orthostatic hypotension with rhythm disturbance, and anticholinergic effects (blurred vision, urinary retention, confusion). Also constipation, dry mouth, sedation, weight gain. Do not give if glaucoma, prostatic hyperplasia, or a cardiac conduction problem |

**St John’s wort** (*Hypericum perforatum*) may help mild to moderate depression. Serious interactions with antidepressants, digoxin, and anticoagulants. **Light therapy:** 5,000–10,000 lux for 30–60 minutes a day, including seasonal patterns.

**ECT** is electrical induction of a seizure. Highly effective for severe episodes and the most rapid effective treatment for major depression in older adults. Consider it when antidepressants fail, are not tolerated, or are medically risky, or when depression itself is compromising health. The slide asks whether modern ECT is painful, causes brain damage, or is risky for older adults, and **does not answer**.

**Suicide:** later-life attempts are more often lethal. Assess history of depression, past attempts, loneliness, family discord, feeling abandoned, recent bereavement, and chronic or severe pain. A **detailed plan plus access to means is high risk**. Act immediately.

## Contrasts that get tested

- Subjective vs perceived vs chronologic vs functional age
- Gerontology vs geriatrics
- Age-related change vs risk factor vs functional consequence
- Competency vs decision-making capacity vs guardianship vs trusteeship
- Pharmacokinetics vs pharmacodynamics; five half-lives
- Polypharmacy is appropriateness, not a number
- Tardive dyskinesia persists; drug-induced parkinsonism can reverse
- RN role in MAiD is aid only
- Delusion vs hallucination vs illusion
- Delirium vs dementia vs the older-adult picture of depression
- CAM rule: 1 and 2, plus 3 or 4
- SSRI first line; amitriptyline, doxepine, and fluoxetine are the named drugs to avoid

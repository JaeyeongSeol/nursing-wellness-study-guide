Types named (Slide 17): Alzheimer, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal. Distinguishing features are **not** printed on the slides. Textbook Table 14-1, paraphrased because it is not on the slide:

- Alzheimer: insidious onset, slowly progressive over about 5 to 10 years; early cognitive change often including memory; later decision-making, language, and behaviour or personality change.

## 2. Risk factors

### Delirium

Slide 5 says “Predisposing and precipitating Risk factors” and does **not** list them. Textbook Chapter 14 does.

**Predisposing** (increase vulnerability): advanced age, dementia, depression, functional dependency, and number of medications.

**Precipitating** (immediate threat): surgery, infections, serious illness, and physical restraints.

The book says risk is highest when several predisposing factors combine with one or more precipitating factors. The slides do not label these modifiable or non-modifiable. Precipitating factors are the ones the delirium-intervention slides tell the nurse to find and treat.

### Dementia

Slide 16 has the heading “Risk factors” and does **not** list them. Textbook section “Factors associated with dementia” does not give a single non-modifiable list such as a gene name. It does say:

- About half of Alzheimer cases worldwide might be attributable to known risk factors.
- Addressing known risk factors might prevent up to one fifth of predicted new cases by 2025.
- There is enough evidence to justify work on exercise, diabetes management, depression treatment, blood pressure control, B vitamins, omega-3 fatty acids, cognitive training, and social activities.
- Behaviours that prevent cardiovascular disease (physical activity, healthy diet) are also associated with better cognitive function.
- Vascular dementia in Table 14-1 is tied to vascular risks (stroke, hypertension).
- Frontotemporal degeneration: family history is common and onset is often before 65.

Do not add other dementia risk factors that are not in these materials.

### Functional consequences

**Delirium** (Slide 8): a medical emergency; longer hospital stays; higher rate of long-term-care residency; short- and long-term functional impairment; development or worsening of dementia. Textbook also says increased mortality and increased dependency, and that delirium predicts later dementia and faster decline in people who already have dementia.

**Dementia** (Slide 19): consequences vary; loss of personhood and self-worth; feeling isolated and depressed; may be hard to recognize or acknowledge. The book says personhood can be understood as increasingly concealed rather than lost.

## 3. High-priority points

- **Comparison table (Slide 28) is the highest-yield slide in this deck.**

| | Delirium | Dementia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Change | Rapid change in mental status | Slow change in mental status |
| Time course | Develops in hours to days | Develops in months to years |
| Course of symptoms | Fluctuate | Progress |
| Outlook | Preventable and treatable | Terminal |
| Nursing focus | Risk factors and symptoms | Safety, caregiver strain, and quality of life |

- **Prevalence figures are on both the slide and the textbook** (Slide 6, book p. 255 as cited): 22% in community-living people (the book text says community-living people with dementia); 15% to 70% in long-term-care residents; 11% to 33% on admission to hospital; an additional 5% to 35% develop delirium after admission; 80% or more in intensive care.
- **Dementia mix** (Slide 18): hard to measure because dementia is a group of diseases; type may not be known until after death. Alzheimer disease 60% to 80%. Vascular dementia 11% to 18% (22% to 34% with Alzheimer disease). Lewy body and Parkinson disease 15% to 20%.
- **CAM: features 1 and 2, plus 3 or 4** (Slide 10).
- **Drug treatment of delirium is not recommended.** Review medications, limit psychoactive drugs to specific symptoms, stop nonessential medications. Benzodiazepines or psychotropic medications are used for delirium associated with alcohol withdrawal (Slide 12).
- **Non-drug delirium care** (Slide 13): physiologic stability and reversible cause; environment; education; communication. Focus (Slide 14): contributing factors, safety, and aggravating factors.
- **Dementia drugs** (Slide 23): most medications stabilize disease process and manage symptoms. Alzheimer disease has specific medications. Underlying cardiac factors for vascular dementia can be treated. Adverse effects are complex. The slide does not name the drugs.
- **Non-drug dementia care** (Slide 24): education, environmental modification, communication, alternative therapies.
- **Acute-care challenge** (Slide 25, citing p. 275): people with dementia are admitted for medical or behavioural problems on top of dementia. Hospital, unfamiliar caregivers, and routine changes worsen dementia-related behaviour. Nurses treat the acute illness and the behaviour.
- **Pain table** (Slide 26), scored 0–2 on each item, total 0–10, higher means greater pain. Items: breathing independent of vocalization; negative vocalization; facial expression; consolability. The slide does not name the tool.
- **Nursing process** (Slide 27): assess factors affecting quality of life; diagnose function, cognition, behaviour, and caregiver; plan for quality of life and function; interprofessional interventions; evaluate quality of life.
- **Take-home** (Slide 30): people with dementia are still people, with stories, character, and individuality, and need human interaction.

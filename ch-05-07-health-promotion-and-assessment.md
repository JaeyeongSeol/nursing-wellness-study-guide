## 2. Risk factors

### Age-related changes versus risk factors (Slides 5–6)

The slides separate these. They do not use the labels “modifiable” and “non-modifiable,” but the definitions support that split.

**Not modifiable — age-related changes** (Slide 6)

- Inevitable, progressive, and irreversible
- Happen to everyone
- Physically degenerative
- Psychological and spiritual side: potential for growth

**Increase vulnerability — risk factors** (Slide 6). The slide does not mark each one modifiable, but they are conditions rather than inevitable aging:

- Diseases
- Environment
- Lifestyle
- Support systems
- Psychosocial circumstances
- Adverse medication effects
- Attitudes based on lack of knowledge

The wellness-model figure (Slide 4) lists risk factors as: pathologic conditions; physiologic and psychologic stressors; environmental barriers; adverse medication effects; ageist attitudes; lack of information.

### Why illness is hard to sort out (Slide 20, citing Hirst, Lane and Miller, 2015, p. 95)

These are reasons assessment is complicated, not a disease list:

- More than one chronic and acute issue is common
- Illness or adverse medication effects tend to be obscure and less predictable
- Every change may have multiple possible causes
- Treatment is often directed at symptoms, not the source
- Cognitive impairments affect accurate reporting
- Myths may lead older adults or support people to attribute treatable conditions to “normal aging”

### Functional decline (Slides 21–22)

The slide asks where older adults experience the most functional decline (hospital, home, long-term care, or equally). It does **not** mark an answer. The next slide states: “Functional decline occurs due to transitions in care.”

Diagram on that transition slide:

- Communication gaps
- Incomplete transfer of information
- Poor client education
- Poor medication-reconciliation procedures
- Unique patient characteristics (for example language barrier, culture)
- Low staffing

### Health-promotion actions that address risk (Slide 7)

- Prevention and screening programs
- Risk-reduction interventions
- Environmental modifications
- Health education

## 3. High-priority points

- **Gerontology versus geriatrics is repeated** (Slide 3, and Chapter 1–2 Slide 13). Gerontology: healthy and successful aging, diversity. Geriatrics: medical problems, shifted from curing to caring, quality of life.
- **The nursing model** (Slide 4 figure) is the spine of these chapters: assess age-related changes, risk factors, and functional consequences; intervene by addressing risk factors, teaching health promotion, and referring; aim for positive functional consequences. Outer ring: consider the whole person and environment; never lose sight of the individual — mind, body, and spirit. The older adult is the person. Health sits at the base of the figure.
- **Negative consequences are the overlap** of age-related changes and risk factors in that figure.
- **Where nurses see older adults** (Slide 8): community, home, nursing home, hospital. The slide also points to the Fraser Health seniors page and the FNHA Elder’s Guide. It does not ask you to memorize the URLs.
- **Continuum of care** (Slide 9) — learn the goal and the nurse’s role in each column:

| Setting | What the slide says | Nurse’s role |
| ------- | ------------------- | ------------ |
| Community | Health promotion, respite, adult day centres | Education, referrals |
| Home care | Goal is rehabilitation. Meal prep, personal care, errands, light housekeeping | Education, referrals, assessment, care planning, hands-on care |
| Nursing home | Needs 24-hour assistance with ADLs | Education, referrals, assessment, care planning, hands-on care, continuous on-site responsibility, coordinates with interprofessionals |

- **Assessments happen in any setting** where an older adult lives (Slide 10).
- **Assessment types** (Slide 11): physical, functional, mental status, comprehensive geriatric, environmental and safety, and “other.”
- **Physical assessment order** (Slide 13): health history before the physical exam; head to toe (observational); vital signs, including orthostatic hypotension. The slide does not define orthostatic hypotension.
- **Functional assessment also covers** sensory function (vision, hearing) and cognitive function as baseline mental status (Slide 14).
- **Interview skills** (Slides 25–26): prepare noise, lighting, comfortable furniture, privacy, and a bathroom; use formal names; explain the purpose; speak directly to the older person; follow that person’s cultural modesty standards; keep interactions under 30 minutes; avoid medical jargon; summarize or restate; prepare the person for the end.
- **Closing point of the deck** (Slide 27): comprehensive assessment and/or geriatric screening tools help reduce poor communication at transitions between hospital and home or long-term care.

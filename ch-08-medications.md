## Summary

### Chapter 8

Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to a drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body at the cell and the target organ. The half-life is the time for the concentration to fall by half, and it takes five half-lives to reach a steady level or to clear the drug after it is stopped. Most oral drugs are absorbed in the small intestine. Aging changes absorption through less stomach acid, a higher gastric pH, slower emptying, and food. Drug action also changes because kidney filtration falls, liver blood flow falls, muscle and body water fall, albumin is often low, and receptors respond differently. Whether the person actually takes the drug depends on motivation, knowledge, culture, the ability to open the container and swallow, and skill with other routes. Polypharmacy is not a pill count. It means more drugs than are clinically needed, judged by whether the combination is appropriate and whether benefit outweighs risk. Older adults feel side effects more strongly. Risk climbs with many drugs, poor nutrition or dehydration, several diseases, heart, kidney, or liver illness, cognitive impairment, past reactions, fever, and a recent change in health. Anticoagulants, antidiabetics, NSAIDs, and CNS drugs need extra caution. Anticholinergics block acetylcholine, worsen cognition over time, and can cancel dementia drugs called cholinesterase inhibitors. The Beers list names drugs that raise delirium and other adverse effects. Avoid them unless benefit is clearly greater, and then use them briefly. Antipsychotics are used often in long-term care. Tardive dyskinesia can start months later and continue after the drug stops. Drug-induced parkinsonism can be mistaken for Parkinson disease and can reverse if the drug is stopped. The nurse reviews every prescription, PRN drug, supplement, herb, and substance, reconciles the list at every transition, and pushes for fewer drugs, clearer teaching, and help from pharmacy, home care, or social work when needed.

## 2. Risk factors

The slides do not label these modifiable or non-modifiable. Age-related body changes are the less modifiable group. Behaviour, prescribing, and monitoring are the group nurses can influence.

### Age-related changes that affect drug action (Slide 6 diagram)

- Decline in renal function (eGFR)
- Hepatic blood flow declines
- Decreased muscle mass and water content
- Low serum albumin (protein)
- Altered receptor sensitivity

Textbook note (Chapter 8): glomerular filtration rate declines from early adulthood by about 1% to 2% per year, which can decrease renal clearance and raise serum levels. Hepatic blood flow declines progressively from about age 40. Less body water and lean tissue, and more body fat, change concentrations of water-soluble and fat-soluble drugs. Low serum albumin increases the active portion of protein-bound drugs. Drugs the book flags when albumin is low or when protein-bound drugs compete include aspirin, digoxin, furosemide, NSAIDs, hypoglycemics, phenytoin, sertraline, and sulfonamides. That drug list is textbook, not on the slide.

### What affects medication-taking behaviour (Slide 7 diagram)

- Motivation
- Knowledge about the medication
- Cultural and psychosocial influences
- Physical ability to remove the substance from the container and administer it
- Ability to swallow oral preparations
- Additional skills for nasal, transdermal, subcutaneous, and other routes

Textbook headings in the same chapter, not copied onto the slide as a list: pathologic processes and functional impairments; behaviours based on myths; communication barriers; lack of information; inappropriate prescribing; polypharmacy and inadequate monitoring; medication nonadherence; cost; insufficient recognition of adverse effects.

### Factors that increase risk of adverse events (Slide 11)

- Higher number of medications
- Malnourishment or dehydration
- Multiple comorbidities
- An illness that interferes with cardiac, renal, or hepatic function
- Cognitive impairment
- History of medication allergies or adverse effects
- Fever that can alter the action of certain medications
- Recent change in health
- Certain medications: anticoagulants/antiplatelets, antidiabetics, NSAIDs, CNS drugs

### Anticholinergic risk (Slide 13)

- OTC products for coughs, colds, flu, and sleep
- Treatments used in older adults: antihistamines, antidepressants, antipsychotics, cardiovascular agents, antiparkinsonian drugs, GI and urinary agents
- Contribute to long-term cognitive impairment
- Can counteract cholinesterase inhibitors, a primary treatment for dementia
- Safer alternatives usually exist

### Functional consequences the slide names (Slide 10 diagram)

- Disease or polypharmacy may alter therapeutic effects
- Adverse effects
- Anticholinergic adverse effects
- Altered mental status
- Antipsychotics in people with dementia
- Tardive dyskinesia and drug-induced parkinsonism

Tardive dyskinesia timing (Slide 15): can begin 3–6 months after antipsychotic initiation and persist after the medication is discontinued.  
Drug-induced parkinsonism (Slide 16): easily misdiagnosed as Parkinson disease, and new medications may be started; manifestations can be reversed if the drug is stopped.

## 3. High-priority points

- **Pharmacokinetics versus pharmacodynamics** (Slide 3). Half-life is the time to drop concentration by half. **Five half-lives** to steady state or to elimination (Slide 4).
- **Polypharmacy is appropriateness, not the count** (Slide 9). Always ask whether benefit outweighs risk. All medications have side effects, and older adults are more likely to have stronger side effects.
- **Beers list** (Slide 17): medications that raise the risk of delirium and other adverse reactions. Do not prescribe them for older adults unless the benefit-risk ratio is greater for that person, and then only for the shortest possible duration.
- **Antipsychotics** (Slide 14): increasingly used in long-term care. In the US, FDA restriction is to psychotic symptoms, not wandering, anxiety, or insomnia. “Not similar in Canada.” The slides say studies support educating nursing staff.
- **Delirium link:** anticholinergics and the Beers list are both tied to cognitive harm. The in-class Beers activity asks for drugs that cause delirium, insomnia (which can trigger delirium through sleep loss), and constipation or GI bleeding (which can also trigger delirium). The drug names are on the Beers PDF, which is not in these slides.
- **Safest use** (Slide 19): medications are safest and most therapeutic when taken as prescribed and reviewed regularly.
- **Assessment goals** (Slide 22): effectiveness of the current regimen; factors that interfere; risks for adverse effects or altered therapeutic action; detect adverse effects; identify teaching needs.
- **Scope of a medication assessment** (Slide 23): regular prescription and OTC drugs, all routes; PRN drugs; vitamins, minerals, supplements; herbs, folk remedies, complementary modalities; alcohol and substances; smoking/inhaling.
- **Nurse interventions from the diagrams** (Slides 26–27): a current medication list; teach appropriate PRN use; adherence devices such as pillboxes and blister packs; nonpharmacologic approaches for behaviour; decrease the number of medications; assess cost (generic versus brand).
- **Interdisciplinary actions** (Slide 28): medication review with the primary physician at every visit; pharmacist reviews actions and interactions; referral to a Seniors Clinic for complex clients; home care if help is needed to administer medications; social worker for financing.

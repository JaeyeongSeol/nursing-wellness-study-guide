## 2. Risk factors

### Elder abuse and neglect (Slide 20)

The slide splits factors by **perpetrator** and **victim**. It does not label them modifiable or non-modifiable.

**Perpetrator**

- Mental illness
- Alcoholism
- Hostility
- Financial dependency on the victim

**Victim**

- Cognitive impairment / dementia
- Problem behaviours
- Disability (physical, communicative)
- Physical dependency
- Financial dependency
- Shared living arrangements
- Isolation or lack of social supports

Textbook note: Chapter 10 also discusses invisibility and vulnerability, caregiver factors, and, in nursing homes, staffing, facility, and resident characteristics. Resident-to-resident aggression is defined in the book and is not on the slides.

### Restraints (Slide 8)

Not a risk-factor list. Associated harms cited from Bradas et al., 2012: increased risk for fractures, delirium, soft tissue injury, and death. The slide says restraints are increasingly seen as limiting autonomy and dignity rather than as safety devices.

### Why abuse is not reported

**Older adults** (Slide 28): do not recognize the behaviour as abusive; do not know where to get help; fear it will get worse; worry about what will happen if it becomes known; feel humiliated; take blame; fear loss of connection; believe family honour is at stake; believe privacy is at stake; unable to express themselves (aphasia, dementia, language barriers, severe illness).

**Staff** (Slide 29). Fear of: revenge, job loss, lack of support, getting someone into trouble, getting involved. Lack of knowledge about: what abuse is, what can be done, the law (mandatory reporting), resources, protocols and procedures.

## 3. High-priority points

- **Five key issues** on the opening diagram (Slide 3): autonomy and rights; advance directives; legal issues specific to long-term care; ethical issues in gerontological nursing; MAiD.
- **Competency is legal. Decision-making capacity is about a particular health decision** (Slide 4). Nurses need to know the guidelines for both.
- **Guardianship versus trusteeship** (Slide 4): partial guardianship keeps limited decisions; full guardianship removes all decision rights; trusteeship is financial capacity.
- **Advance directives are made while the person is competent**, for a future time when they cannot decide or communicate (Slide 5). Practice examples: MOST, and representation agreement/living will.
- **Long-term care rights in Canada are mainly provincial/territorial.** A recurring ethical tension is safety versus freedom (Slide 6).
- **Ethical issues named** (Slide 7 diagram): use of restraints; artificial nutrition and hydration.
- **MAiD nursing role** (Slide 14 diagram): three roles exist — determining eligibility, providing MAiD, and aiding in the provision of MAiD. The registered nurse’s role is limited to aiding a physician or nurse practitioner. RNs are not allowed to prescribe, compound, dispense, or administer any substance intended for MAiD. The eligibility checklist itself is **not** on the slides.
- **Nurse’s role in legal and ethical issues** (Slide 16): promote advance care planning; implement advance directives; facilitate decisions about care; promote caregiver wellness.
- **Abuse types to be able to match to a definition** (Slide 21): physical, sexual, emotional/psychological, exploitation, neglect, abandonment, self-neglect. Spiritual abuse is an added slide (Slide 25).
- **Physical-abuse examples** on the diagram (Slide 22): pinching, spitting, environmental restraints, medications, forcible handling, pulling hair, pushing, shoving, hitting, slapping, poking, biting.
- **Emotional-abuse examples** (Slide 24 diagram): not considering wishes; not respecting belongings; speaking another language in front of the resident; inappropriate control of activities; treating the resident like a child; bullying; name-calling; ignoring or disregarding behaviours. Possible indicators (same slide): low self-esteem, extreme tiredness, nervousness around a particular person, depression, anxiety, increased isolation, fear, withdrawal.
- **Reporting** (Slides 30–31): mandatory reporting does not require the reporter to know that abuse occurred, only to report suspicion. Verification rests with the public agency (for example police). Investigation of reports usually occurs in 24–72 hours. Signs include bruises, welts, or fractures, and neglect signs such as frostbite, dehydration, oversedation, mental changes, and uncontrolled medical conditions. Most laws give immunity to mandatory reporters who act in good faith without malicious intent. BCCNM has a Duty to Report Standard based on the Health Professions Act. Reporting is described as everyone’s moral responsibility and the nurse’s professional legal responsibility.
- **Management point** (Slide 31): legal and ethical challenges are often best managed with early, open communication. The nurse provides information and support and refers for further help.

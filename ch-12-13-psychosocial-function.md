## Summary

### Chapter 12

Psychosocial health covers cognition and affect. Cognition is thinking and reasoning, and its impairments include delirium and dementia. Affect is life inside the self and with other people, and its impairments include depression, psychiatric illness, and losing touch with reality. Later life brings major events: retirement, moving, chronic illness, widowhood, deaths of friends and family, ageist attitudes, and lost autonomy such as driving. Stress is the sum of everything acting on the body, pleasant or not, and it moves through alarm, resistance, and exhaustion. Stress runs high when health, function, money, supports, or coping skills are poor, or when several hassles and life events pile up. Coping is either problem-focused or emotion-focused, for example seeking information, reframing, exercise, or asking for reassurance. Resilience is the ability to recover and to use the wisdom gained from adversity. It is tied to spirituality, hope, and meaning, and the nurse’s role is to support it. Spirituality usually becomes broader and more personal with age and is linked with better health. Culture shapes how psychosocial health is understood. Health literacy, the ability to listen, speak one’s needs, and act on written instructions, is not proficient for 6 in 10 adult Canadians, especially immigrants.

### Chapter 13

Assessment looks at mental status, appearance, movement, social skill, decision-making, mood, contact with reality, supports, and religion. The MMSE covers five areas: orientation, attention, memory, language, and spatial-visual skills. A delusion is a fixed false belief. A hallucination is a sensation with nothing outside causing it. An illusion is a misreading of something that is really there. Withdrawal and gestures toward empty space can signal hallucinations. Nursing diagnoses include low self-esteem, powerlessness, social isolation, and ineffective coping, and also readiness to strengthen coping, resilience, or spiritual well-being. Interventions protect dignity, avoid elderspeak, give real choices, restore meaningful roles, invite reminiscence, build supports, and address spiritual needs. The work has helped if the person reports a better view of self, takes part in decisions, feels some control, and uses effective coping.

## 2. Risk factors

### Life events that challenge psychosocial health (Slide 6)

The slide does not call these modifiable or non-modifiable. They are major changes:

- Retirement
- Relocation
- Chronic illness and functional impairment
- Widowhood
- Death of friends and family
- Ageist attitudes
- Loss of autonomy (for example driving)

### Factors that contribute to high levels of stress (Slide 7)

- Poor physical health
- Impaired functional abilities
- Weak social supports
- Lack of economic resources
- Immature developmental level
- Narrow range of coping skills
- Occurrence of unanticipated events
- Several daily hassles at the same time
- Several major life events over a short time

### Factors that affect psychosocial function (Slide 11 diagram)

Spirituality, culture, support system, and resilience. These are influences, not automatically risks. The resilience slide treats resilience as something the nurse supports.

### Support-system risks (Slide 18)

Limited literacy skills influence understanding of health information. The slide states 6/10 adult Canadians do not have proficient health literacy, especially immigrants.

## 3. High-priority points

- **Cognitive versus affective** (Slide 3). Cognitive: thinking and reasoning; delirium and dementia. Affective: inside the self and between the person and others; depression, psychiatric illness, loss of touch with reality.
- **Nurses promote psychosocial wellness by helping build coping** (Slide 4). Challenges come from compromised health and function, and from role change, relationships, and living environments.
- **Stress stages: alarm, resistance, exhaustion** (Slide 8). Coping is problem-focused or emotion-focused (Slide 9). Coping examples on that diagram: seeking information; reframing; hopeful outlook; stress-reduction techniques; physical activity; fantasies about outcomes; reassurance and emotional support; identifying limits and realistic goals; art, music, and writing.
- **A true/false slide asks** whether health-enhancing effects of religiosity are due to the unique social support of church networks (Slide 16). The deck does **not** mark true or false. Do not guess an answer that is not on the slide.
- **Spirituality and religious practices of older people are linked to positive health outcomes.** Professionals should know the person’s beliefs and what function they serve (Slide 15). Three reflection questions are on Slide 14 (meaning, strength for daily difficulty, understanding life’s difficulties), from the Canadian Community Health Survey citation on the slide.
- **Culture influences how a person perceives psychosocial function** (Slide 17).
- **Psychosocial assessment domains** (Slides 21 and 24): mental status (for example MMSE); physical appearance; decision-making and executive function; affective function; contact with reality; social support; religion and spirituality.
- **Mental status observations beyond the MMSE five areas** (Slide 22): physical appearance; motor function, body language, psychomotor behaviour; social skills; response to the interview; orientation; alertness and attention; memory; speech and language; calculation and higher language skills.
- **Contact-with-reality questions** (Slide 26): “Do you have any thought that you can’t seem to get rid of?” “Do you sometimes hear voices when you’re alone?” “Do you sometimes think you see things that other people don’t see?” Nonverbal clues to hallucination: extreme withdrawal and isolation; contentment with social isolation; gestures that normally occur in response to perceived stimuli.
- **Textbook on executive function** (Chapter 13): multifaceted abilities including cognitive flexibility, concept formation, and self-monitoring; not necessarily memory impairment. Deficits can appear early in dementia, especially with frontal-lobe disease, and a person may score well on the MMSE and still be unsafe in daily activities. Indicators: less mental flexibility, limited abstract thinking, difficulty with problem solving, decline in conceptualizing, trouble adapting, difficulty shifting from one idea to another. This detail is textbook, not written out on the slide.
- **Nursing diagnoses named** (Slide 27): situational low self-esteem (or risk); powerlessness (or risk); social isolation; ineffective coping; readiness for enhanced spiritual well-being; readiness for enhanced coping; readiness for enhanced resilience.
- **Outcome examples** (Slide 28): psychosocial adjustment to life change; self-esteem; adaptation to physical disability; body image; grief resolution.
- **Interventions** (Slides 29–30): enhancing self-esteem (verbal and nonverbal communication; avoid infantilization and elderspeak); promoting control (information, rephrasing, address threats such as lack of privacy and loss of individuality); involving the person in decisions; addressing role loss by identifying meaningful roles; reminiscence; fostering social supports; spiritual needs (caring and compassion, referral, religious activities); teaching healthy aging. The diagram also lists healthy aging classes, life review, and improving functional abilities. The group activity says not to include healthy aging classes in that exercise.
- **Evaluation** (Slide 31): positive self-perceptions; involvement in decisions; expressed feelings of control; effective coping strategies.
- **Nurse’s role in resilience** (Slide 19): support resilience.
